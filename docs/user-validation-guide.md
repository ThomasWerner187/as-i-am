# A small, genuine user-validation session

**Status: a worksheet for future use. No participant session or finding is recorded here.**
The aim is to learn whether the available choices help the individual participant, and where
they create new barriers. A model acting as a persona is not a participant.

## Before inviting anyone

Invite adults who actually use access settings or assistive technology relevant to the task.
Explain that this is a fictional booking prototype, what participation involves and that there
are no real payments or reservations. Ask what would make the session accessible to them;
do not ask for a diagnosis or medical proof. Use their preferred terminology.

Agree on a short session and flexible breaks, their preferred device/input method, and any
compensation before beginning. Make clear that participation and questions are optional and
that they may stop without explaining why. Obtain separate permission for recording and for
any quotation or screenshot shared beyond the session.

Do not ask someone to imitate tremor, blur their vision, disable their assistive technology
or struggle through an inaccessible baseline to produce a dramatic comparison. They may start
with the presentation and equipment they normally use. If a task becomes tiring or inaccessible,
stop or adapt the session rather than treating persistence as success.

WAI's [Stories of Web Users](https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/user-stories/) are useful
background on differing experiences, not a substitute for recruiting or listening to real people.
Do not reuse a published persona as a participant identity.

## A flexible 15–20 minute session

Start with: “We are checking the design, not testing you. Please tell us when the interface
gets in your way. You can skip any task, use your usual tools, or stop.”

Offer these tasks and let the participant choose a manageable subset. Avoid demonstrating
the desired path first. Read task instructions aloud if requested.

1. Find two adjacent seats you would be happy with. Explain what the selection and price mean.
2. Try any of **Make pointing easier for me**, **Make reading easier for me** and **Give me less to process** that interest you.
   Combine, change or remove choices. Explore how to return to the original presentation.
3. Inspect a ticket review. Decide whether you want to confirm the fictional tickets.
4. If continuing, open the restaurant and inspect what carrying preferences changes. Ask what
   information they think was shared; compare that answer with the actual interface disclosure.
5. Explore the full menu and focused choices. Use fictional food requirements, not medical history.
6. Try **Prepare for me** if they want delegated help. Ask them to inspect the proposal and
   identify what remains theirs to decide.

If comparing two views, record the order and any coaching. Repeating the same task introduces
familiarity; a quicker second attempt does not by itself prove an accessibility improvement.
There is no required completion time. Stop if the participant wants to stop.

## Neutral prompts

- “What are you trying to do here?”
- “What do you expect this control to do?”
- “What, if anything, changed in a helpful or unhelpful way?”
- “Was there information or an option you wanted but could not find?”
- “Which decisions felt like yours? Did anything change unexpectedly?”
- “What would you keep, change or remove?”

Avoid asking “Was this easier?” repeatedly or suggesting that the participant should prefer
adaptation. Disliking a change, using the original view or declining delegation is useful evidence.

Record whether the participant completed, declined or could not continue a task, and why if
known. Label their words as a quote only if recorded accurately and permission permits use.
A facilitator's impression is not a participant statement. Browser measurements belong in a
separate technical record; they do not measure a person's comfort or independence.

## Report only what happened

Use a modest report structure:

- Participants: actual count, relevant voluntarily described access context and recruitment limits.
- Setting: tested build, devices, task order and accommodations.
- Observations: specific tasks, selected settings, assistance and outcomes.
- Feedback: permitted quotations or clearly marked paraphrases.
- Changes to investigate: each linked to an observed problem or request.
- Limits: small sample, incomplete tasks, learning effects and untested access methods.

Do not turn one positive session into a claim about disabled people generally, or publish a
percentage that suggests a representative sample. Do not claim clinical benefit, reduced fatigue,
WCAG conformance or independent interoperability from this exercise. State disagreement and
negative feedback alongside positive observations.

Share any proposed public quotation with the participant for confirmation. Thank them, honor
agreed compensation, and explain which feedback will be investigated. If no sessions take place,
leave this worksheet blank and continue to state that user research is pending.
