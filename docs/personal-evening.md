# A night for two

The current story starts with an ordinary wish: a film and dinner with someone you love.
Alex and Lea are fictional. They have explicitly shared preferences with their agent;
the prototype supplies that example history instead of claiming a real memory service.

Alex asks for his familiar calm view because of a migraine **today**. Next week's visit is a
separate plan. The agent sends display settings, not the health message, to participating
websites. Dark appearance, lower glare and stopped animations reflect Alex's chosen settings;
they are not a universal migraine treatment or an accessibility certification.

## The visible journey

1. Open the home page. **Saved preferences** shows the source of the fictional context.
2. **Plan our evening** applies the calm profile and finds next week's Friday showing at
   20:15. Alex is at the aisle, with Lea immediately inside. Dinner availability is checked
   before the film proposal is confirmed.
3. **One row further back** changes F1–F2 to G1–G2 and preserves that arrangement.
   The person reviews and confirms the demo tickets in the cinema interface.
4. **Dinner, next** reads the confirmed cinema date/time. OLIVA receives the functional
   display receipt separately from the dinner request. The 18:00 table leaves 90 minutes
   for dinner and 15 minutes to walk, arriving at 19:45 for the 20:15 film.
5. Three source-backed recipe options are shown visually. Mushroom risotto is first because
   it is Alex's favorite and passes the requested ingredient filter. Lea's explicitly shared
   peanut and avocado exclusions remain applied. Cross-contact still needs kitchen confirmation.
6. The person can inspect the full menu, compare the original appearance, and confirm the
   table in the visible restaurant interface. Both bookings are synthetic.

The home walkthrough is a labelled preset, not an embedded autonomous model. A real external
agent can read `get_personal_evening_context` and perform corresponding native WebMCP actions
on the direct cinema and restaurant pages. The advanced access-choice journey remains at
`/guided`, including pointing, reading and cognitive support combinations.

## Native request for the fictional scenario

> Read the supplied Alex and Lea demonstration context. Plan a movie night next week, with
> dinner first. Discover each participating site's tools and adaptation capabilities. Apply
> Alex's explicitly requested calm view for today's planning. Select next Friday at 20:15,
> check that dinner can fit, and propose adjacent seats with Alex at the aisle and Lea inside.
> Let Alex move a row back while preserving that arrangement. Stage a review and leave ticket
> confirmation to the person. Then read the confirmed date and time, transfer only the
> functional display receipt to OLIVA, and propose an available quiet table before the film.
> Show three menu recommendations, prioritizing mushroom risotto only if it meets the explicit
> peanut and avocado exclusions. Distinguish listed ingredients, missing information and
> cross-contact. Keep full menu access and final confirmation with the person. These are
> fictional preferences and reservations, not facts about the person testing the prototype.

## Film direction

The new cut is planned for 112 seconds, with the first spoken request at second 7. Two voices
share 101 words. Actual browser images carry the explanation: the view changes, two adjacent
outside seats move together, a dinner time fits the movie, and three dishes replace a long list.
Short captions sit outside the captured interface. Technical detail belongs in the evidence
and testing guide rather than competing with the person's task on screen.

See [recording status](recording.md) for the completed export and checks; planned timing alone
does not establish a finished film. Artwork provenance is in [menu images](art-sources/menu-images.md).

## The pitch

> Your agent remembers what matters. The website responds in a way you can use.
> You plan the evening together.

Inclusion is the reason for the contract: people should be able to choose support, change their
minds and participate in ordinary activities without re-explaining every preference on every
page. The next validation step is research with disabled people, on their own devices and with
their own access tools. Technical tests and a fictional scenario are not a substitute for that.
